Ethical issues and potential improvements

AI can present ethical issues by design. It will acquire patterns from humans if using human content. If the model is intended to be fluent then it will appear certain where it is mistaken. If the model is trained on conversational content, then it relies on human labour and personal data. The main issues that highlight the divergence between legal and ethical standards are discussed below.

Bias

Evidence that all users are served equally by these systems is limited. In an experiment, 30 cities were used where users interacted with smart speakers and asked ten questions (Uswitch, 2020). The experiment was conducted by a commercial company where understanding was assessed using Google Transcribe and the ranking also uses search volume; a poor indicator of failure. The Centre for Data Ethics and Innovation (2020) highlights the issues associated with evidence for bias. There is limited clarity surrounding whether algorithms are more harmful than human actions. Without evidence of which users are misunderstood then the failures of the systems remain hidden. Acquiring this evidence requires data about protected attributes.

Privacy

Snap,s My AI highlights the divergence between the legal and ethical. In October 2023 the ICO provisionally found that Snap had failed to carry out a privacy risk assessment prior to deployment of the Chabot including risks to users aged 13 to 17 (ICO, 2023). This is important because under the ICO guidance (2020) impact assessment is the core requirement. However the ICO has not made a finding about any breach of the law. The guidance only applies to personal data. What is not included in this is whether a user aged 13 or under was made aware of what data was stored by the Chabot

Misinformation

Fluency does not equate to factual accuracy. Responses from four different AI systems, across 14 languages and over 3000 responses were examined by journalists from 22 public service broadcasters. 45% of these responses contained at least one important error (BBC, 2025). While this does highlight a problem for the broadcasters the scope and language coverage makes this a small sample unlikely. The House of Lords Committee (2024) has proposed the issue. AI systems are trained on plausibility and probability rather than fact. The consequence of this is reflected in the law. In Al-Haroun 18 of 45 referenced cases do not exist (Ayinde v Haringey [2025] EWHC 1383 (Admin)) and the Divisional Court has also highlighted the issue that publicly available systems are unsuitable for carrying out reliable legal research.

Hidden labour

Labor is involved in both the training and interaction data. A whistleblower for the Guardian revealed that contractors for Siri would often hear criminal and medical information (Hern, 2019). Apple responded that less than 1% of interactions were inspected and that none of these were linked to Apple IDs. There is a Fairwork rating for AI in procurement (Fairwork, 2025). The examples given above highlight practice rather than prevalence.

Realistic improvements

There are three improvements that can be considered and ordered by likely implementation. Standards are first as highlighted by the House of Lords Committee (2024) who have recommended common auditing and use of accredited standards. The issue with guidance alone was highlighted in the case of Ayinde. A second improvement is legal clarity regarding measurement of bias. This is inexpensive and unlocks the data required for effective auditing. The final improvement is procurement ratings similar to Fairwork. This is the least effective option as it is voluntary. The Department for Science, Innovation and Technology (2023) opted not to introduce new legislation. However Snap highlights that the ICO only acted following deployment. What role does regulation play in this? The Department for Science, Innovation and Technology (2023) opted not to introduce new legislation. The ICO only acted following deployment and this was only provisionally. Standards would allow for pre-deployment auditing by regulators. Without standards fairness would remain aspirational.
